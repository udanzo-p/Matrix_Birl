NuFlow by BirlaNu · opportunity sizing and prioritisation · qualitative framework · November 2026

Win at home with what is ready, bet abroad where polymer is already allowed.

A geography × product-category matrix scored on a rubric designed for this decision: seven criteria with anchored scales, weights you can change, two gates, and four calls on every cell (quick win, strategic bet, follow-on, deprioritise). Adjacencies are scored on their own, coarser scale. Every call is a hypothesis with a test and a kill criterion, not a verdict.

What the model says

    Top quick wins

    Ready product, open channel, entry in under 12 months. Ranked by combined score.

    Top strategic bets

    Attractive but gated by certification, channel or product work. Worth a capped investment to de-risk.

    How to read this

    • No hard data was supplied for most cells. Scores combine sourced market facts (growth, certification, prices, networks) with analyst judgement. The Evidence tab tags every input; each cell shows a confidence level.
    • Each call is a hypothesis to be tested. The Test plan tab gives the cheapest test that would confirm or kill it, with a budget and time box.
    • Robustness is shown, not assumed. Each cell's call is re-run under 300 random weight sets (each weight ±50%). A cell marked 90% keeps its call in nine of ten.
    • Change the rubric, not the answer. If you disagree, move the weights or thresholds on the Rubric tab; the matrix, portfolio and slide notes update.
    Scoring rubric · designed for a polymer-hybrid entrant

    Seven criteria, two axes, two gates

    The brief names five criteria. The rubric adds two: addressable size, because "opportunity sizing" needs a sense of scale, and right to win, because a market can be attractive and open while NuFlow still cannot serve it. Criteria are grouped into an attractiveness axis (is the prize worth it?) and an ease axis (can a new entrant get it?). Every criterion is scored 1 to 5 against written anchors, so two people scoring the same cell should land within a point.

    Weights

    Within each axis, weights sum to the axis. Defaults favour price realisation and channel access, the two criteria the 18% EBITDA plan is most sensitive to.

    Classification rules and thresholds

    Gates. A cell cannot be a quick win if regulatory ease is 2 or less (certification takes over a year) or channel access is 2 or less (no route without a major investment). Gates stop a high average hiding one blocking criterion.

    Why this rubric

    Design choiceReason
    1–5 anchored scales, not free scoringAnchors tie a score to an observable fact (a CAGR band, a certification time, a gross-margin band), so scores can be challenged with evidence instead of opinion.
    Ease criteria scored so that 5 is easyRegulatory complexity and competitive intensity are reversed, so every criterion reads "higher is better" and the axes can be averaged without sign errors.
    Two axes instead of one totalA single total hides whether a cell is good because it is easy or because it is big. Quick wins and strategic bets differ exactly on that split.
    Price realisation anchored on gross marginThe plan's gate G0 needs at least 50% gross margin per SKU to clear 18% EBITDA. Bands are set around that line using the hero mixer's ₹1,299 landed cost.
    Size on a log scalePools range from USD 5 mn to USD 2 bn. A linear scale would make every export market look identical and every India cell look small; each step up is roughly three times the one below.
    Base score plus adjustmentSome criteria are mostly geographic (regulation, channel), some mostly category-driven (price, competition). Each cell starts from its geography's score, adds its category's adjustment, then applies a stated override where the combination behaves differently (shattafs in the GCC, bidet sprayers in the US).
    Gates on regulation and channelThese two are the only criteria that can stop entry outright. Averages cannot be allowed to compensate for them.
    Confidence and robustness shown separatelyConfidence says how good the evidence is; robustness says how much the call depends on the weights. A low-confidence, low-robustness quick win is the first thing to test.
    A different, coarser scale for adjacenciesAdjacencies are go / pilot / park decisions with few options and a different risk profile (customer concentration, payment terms, brand dilution). A 0–3 scale with explicit risk penalties avoids false precision there.

    OEM and private label, government tenders, hospitality bulk supply
